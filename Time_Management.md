# Time Management

These are some principles that I have found useful when allocating my time in both work and life in general.
I don't claim that any of these are novel; somebody has probably already said them in books that I haven't read.

These principles are not orthogonal and all distill down to prioritization, but this is how I like to think about them.

## The value of your time

My dad taught me this lesson a long time ago.
He used to change the oil in his car, but it became increasingly tedious due to modern car designs.
At the same time, there were service providers who could do it for $30, including the hazardous waste processing.
It took about an hour to change the oil and my dad decided his time was worth more than $30/hour, particularly on the weekend, so even though he could change the oil, it made economic sense to outsource it once he assigned an appropriate value to his time.

It is often true that young people have plenty of time and limited money, while older people have the opposite problem.
The trick is recognize when you have transitioned from one to the other and start spending money to buy back your time.

This one is particularly difficult if you were raised to be frugal or developed that on your own when you were in the "unlimited time, limited money" category.
Why should I spend money for something that I can do myself?
Because your time is not free.
It doesn't matter if you are paid $10/hour or $1000/hour, your time has value.
The key to not feeling guilty about this is to compute the actual value of your time and remember that number the next time you're tempted to waste a Saturday doing something that could be done by a professional at reasonable cost.

Obviously, everyone who reads this has finite money (if any billionaires read this: pay taxes, *******), so at some point you can't outsource _everything_, but lots of folks are spending their valuable time on the wrong things.

## Zero-based budgeting (ZBB)

ZBB is the difference between how governments and companies allocate money.
You can read about the details [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-based_budgeting).
In the context of time, you make a stack ranking of the priorities in your life and you do the "pour" until you run out of time.  What is below the line, you just stop doing, rather than trying to keep doing everything forever.

Over the years, I have declared some of extracurricular activities "below the line".
It was hard to imagine ceasing to do them, given that I did them for many years (when I had more free time), but I realized that I had to make hard choices in order to focus on things that mattered more.

One of the hardest cuts I've had to make recently is to stop working on NWChem.
NWChem was the subject of my PhD research and is the foundation of my entire career in HPC.
It's one of the most important things I've ever work on, both in terms of its impact on the scientific community but my own development.
However, NWChem is no longer relevant to my job and therefore I have to allocate time for it from my personal time budget.
NWChem is below the line, so that's it.  It's not that my feelings about NWChem changed; rather, other things moved above it in the stack ranking of my life and how I spend my time.

## Do-nothing hypothesis

(This is probably related to the null hypothesis of statistics, but it's not exactly the same, and somebody will probably get mad if I call it the null hypothesis.)

The biggest mistake I made early in my career was confusing "I can help here" with "I should help here."
When you have broadly useful skills, say in MPI, there are many opportunities to apply them.
It is tempting to look for opportunities to help people by applying your skills to their problems.
It feels great to be able to help people and sometimes they have express gratitude when you do.
However, at some point, the opportunities exceed the time you have and it's time to do nothing.

When your time is limited, always ask yourself, "what happens if I ignore this problem?"
It feels cruel, if you are an empathetic person and have skills that would help others, but not every HPC software problem needs to be fixed, particularly by you.
In some cases, not helping people forces them to figure it out the hard way and it makes them better.

If you really feel bad about doing nothing, you can appease your guilt by writing documentation.
